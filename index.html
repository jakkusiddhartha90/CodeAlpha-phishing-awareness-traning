01 / UNDERSTAND THE THREAT

What is phishing?

Phishing is a type of cyberattack in which an attacker pretends to be a trusted person or organization to trick someone into revealing information, opening a harmful attachment, or visiting a fraudulent website.

✉

Email phishing

Fraudulent emails imitate banks, companies, delivery services, or colleagues to steal information or make you click unsafe links.

🎯

Spear phishing

A targeted message tailored to a specific person or organization, often using personal or workplace details to appear believable.

▣

Smishing

Phishing through SMS or messaging apps. Messages may claim a parcel is delayed or an account needs urgent attention.

☎

Vishing

Voice-call scams in which someone impersonates support staff, a bank, or an authority to pressure victims into sharing secrets.

⌘

Whaling

A highly targeted attack aimed at senior executives or other high-value individuals, often involving payments or sensitive data.

◎

Website spoofing

A fake website is designed to look like a real service. A misleading domain may be used to collect login details.

02 / DETECT THE WARNING SIGNS

Pause. Check. Verify.

One warning sign does not always prove a message is malicious. Look for several clues, then verify through a trusted channel.

01

Unexpected urgency or threats

“Your account will close in 10 minutes” is designed to make you act without thinking.

⏱
02

Unusual sender address

Check the complete email address, not just the display name. Small spelling changes can be deceptive.

✉
03

Suspicious links or domains

Hover over links on a computer or inspect the destination carefully. Watch for misspellings and unrelated domains.

↗
04

Unexpected attachments

Do not open unexpected files, especially executable files or documents asking you to enable macros.

📎
05

Requests for secrets or payment

Legitimate support teams should not ask you to disclose your password or one-time passcode.

🔑
06

Unexpected or odd language

Generic greetings, unusual wording, or a request that does not fit the sender's normal behavior deserve a closer look.

⚑
03 / LEARN WITH EXAMPLES

Would you spot it?

These fictional examples are for awareness training. They do not use real organizations, links, or credentials.

EXAMPLE A · SUSPICIOUS
✉   Mail preview •••
From: Account Support <support@secure-example.invalid>
Subject: Immediate action required!
Dear Customer,

Your account will be permanently suspended today. Confirm your password and OTP immediately using the button below.
VERIFY ACCOUNT NOW
  • Generic greeting
  • Threat and artificial urgency
  • Requests password and OTP
⚠ Likely phishing — do not interact.
EXAMPLE B · SAFER PROCESS
✉   Mail preview •••
From: Known colleague (verify address)
Subject: Review the meeting notes
Hello,

Here are the meeting notes we discussed. I have also saved a copy in our organization's usual document workspace. Please open it from the official workspace if you need to review it.
OPEN OFFICIAL WORKSPACE
  • Context matches expected work
  • Uses a known, approved channel
  • Still verify sender and destination
✓ More trustworthy process — verify first.
Important: A polished message can still be malicious, and a poorly written message is not automatically phishing. Verify unexpected requests independently.
04 / UNDERSTAND THE HUMAN FACTOR

Social engineering: the human hack.

Social engineering manipulates people into taking actions or sharing information. Attackers often exploit emotions rather than technical weaknesses.

⏳

Urgency

Creates pressure to act immediately without checking.

🏛

Authority

Pretends to be a manager, bank, or government official.

🎁

Curiosity or reward

Uses prizes, shocking news, or tempting offers.

🤝

Trust

Impersonates a colleague, friend, or service provider.

05 / BUILD SAFE HABITS

Your everyday defense plan.

Good security habits reduce the chance that one deceptive message becomes a serious incident.

01

Verify independently. Contact the person or organization using a known phone number or official website.

02

Use multi-factor authentication. Enable MFA and never share approval codes or one-time passwords.

03

Use unique passwords. Use a reputable password manager and never reuse work passwords.

04

Keep software updated. Install updates from official sources and use supported security tools.

05

Report suspicious messages. Follow your organization's reporting process; do not forward suspicious links casually.

06

Act quickly if you clicked. Report it to IT/security, change exposed passwords from a trusted device, and follow incident-response guidance.

🛡
REMEMBER

Stop. Think. Verify. Report.

If a message feels unusual, slow down. Use a trusted channel to confirm it before taking action.
